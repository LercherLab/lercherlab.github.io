Lercher Lab

Inflammatory Signal Integration and Inflammatory Memory

We study how immune cells integrate inflammatory signals and remember them, building a clearer picture of immunity in the real world.

Learn more ↗

Research

The Problem

Humans encounter a plethora of microbes daily. Innate immune cells are at the first responders to fend off disease caused by pathogenic microbes but can also cause life-threatening hyperinflammatory dysregulation. While pathogen-associated molecular patterns and/or cytokines are prime inducers of inflammatory responses, cell intrinsic and extrinsic factors including cell identity, tissue environment and even diet influence innate responses. Another regulatory layer of innate immune responses are past inflammatory encounters that result in durable epigenetic memory. This innate immune memory impacts secondary inflammatory responses and thereby affects disease pathology. There is a significant gap in our understanding of how cells integrate a myriad of immunological and/or environmental cues to mount adequate responses and elicit memory. Mapping the underlying molecular networks governing this complex yet fundamental cellular ability is a major frontier in biomedical research, with the potential to reveal novel concepts of immune regulation that will inform therapeutic strategies.

The Approach

In the Lercher lab, we take a holistic approach to decipher the black box of inflammatory signal integration and resulting epigenetic memory. We employ tractable primary cell culture systems and complementary murine infection models to study real-world pathogen encounters. We utilize next generation sequencing, real-time imaging, genetic engineering and immune profiling to build a framework of complex inflammatory signal integration and memory formation.

The Questions

  1. 01How do cells integrate inflammatory cues to mount adequate immune responses?
  2. 02What are the determinants of cell-type or tissue-specific immune responses?
  3. 03How do innate immune responses lead to durable epigenetic memory?
  4. 04Which cellular and cross-organ communication networks shape innate immunity and memory?
  5. 05Can we use synthetic immunology to build circuits that control complex immune responses?

Alexander Lercher

Group Leader

Alex is from Austria and did his PhD with Andreas Bergthaler at CeMM in Vienna, followed by postdoct in Charlie Rice's lab at Rockefeller University. He now leads his own group at CIID Heidelberg, where he studies how immune cells make sense of complex inflammatory signals during infection and form epigenetic memory.
